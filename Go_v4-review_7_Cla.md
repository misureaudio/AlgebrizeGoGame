**Where the rigor still has a real gap — Lemma 4.2.** The combinatorial argument (two disjoint eyes, opponent fills at most one per move, so a liberty always survives) is correct and is the standard proof that two-eyed groups can't be captured. But the proof then asserts "Consequently... $f(c) \supseteq c$" as though that combinatorial fact automatically satisfies the *stated* definition of Benson's operator — "liberties lie entirely within regions enclosed by $X$." That phrase "enclosed by $X$" is never pinned down formally anywhere in either document; it's doing exactly the load-bearing work that "candidate eye via $H_1$" was flagged for doing loosely in the previous review. The gap is small but real: you have a correct informal capture-avoidance argument and a correct fixed-point theorem, and a sentence gluing them together that isn't itself derived from the formal definition of $f$.

The fix is concrete and not hard: define "enclosed by $X$" the way Benson's algorithm actually computes it — a region $R$ (connected component of $V_\emptyset$) is enclosed by $X$ iff every vertex adjacent to $R$ lies in $X$, i.e., $R$'s coboundary (in the $\delta_0$ sense you already built in §3) touches no stone outside $X$. Once "enclosed" is *that* — a flood-fill condition stated in the same coboundary language as liberties — Lemma 4.2's proof becomes: the two eyes are enclosed regions by construction (orthogonal-surround), disjointness means no single opponent move destroys both, hence $c$'s liberties (which lie in these two regions, by hypothesis) remain nonempty under any $X \supseteq c$, hence $f(X) \supseteq c$ for such $X$, hence $f(c) \supseteq c$ directly from the formal definition rather than from an appeal to intuition. That's maybe four lines to add and it closes the one place where V4 still asks the reader to trust a leap.

**One more small thing worth fixing while you're in there:** the eye definition in §3 writes $\partial_1^* \mathbf{1}_{\{v\}} = 0$ in $C_1(G_c;\mathbb{Z}_2)$ — but $\mathbf{1}_{\{v\}}$ is a 0-cochain, its coboundary (whatever $\partial_1^*$ is meant to denote — presumably $\delta_0$) lands in $C^1$, not $C_1$. Same species of slip as the original §4 type error, just recurring in a new spot. Since you've already built $\delta_0$ correctly earlier in the same section, just reuse it: $v$ is a candidate eye of $c$ iff $\delta_0(\mathbf{1}_{\{v\}})$ is supported entirely on edges into $c$ (all four — or fewer, at the boundary — neighbors of $v$ lie in $c$). That's consistent with your own machinery and avoids introducing an undefined operator.

**Section 5 (CGT) has a smaller overclaim worth softening.** You correctly flag that ko threats and shared liberties break independence, but then say the fix is refining to "a sum of games with a temperature parameter." Temperature/thermography is about optimal move timing in hot games with well-defined disjunctive structure — it doesn't actually restore independence when a ko threat in one region changes the *legal move set or outcome* in another. That's a genuinely different kind of interaction (loopy games / ko as a shared resource across the sum), and it's still open territory in CGT-for-Go, not something "temperature" resolves. I'd change this to something like: "such interactions require treating the position as a single non-decomposable loopy game, or modeling ko threats as a separate resource pool exchanged across regions — outside the scope of the direct-sum picture given here." Less tidy, but accurate, and it matches the honest tone you've earned in Remark 3.2 and Remark 4.3.
