# Question: Some expanded view about what we could possibly verify with LEAN4

The new file adds two genuinely completed, `sorry`-free proofs — and one of them has an important hidden trade-off worth being precise about before calling it "Lemma 4.2 verified."

**`superko_is_dag` — clean, complete, and actually a nice generalization.** This formalizes Prop 2.1's acyclicity half. Walking through it: `history_length_increases` is trivial (list length under `::` strictly increases), `path_increases_length` inducts correctly over `Relation.TransGen`'s two constructors (`single`/`tail`), chaining strict inequalities via transitivity, and `superko_is_dag` closes by deriving `a.history.length < a.history.length` from an assumed self-loop, contradicting `Nat.lt_irrefl`. This is sound. Notice what it *doesn't* need: `BoardState n`'s finiteness never enters the argument — acyclicity here is purely a consequence of history-monotonicity, exactly the thing I flagged back when reviewing Prop 2.1's prose proof ("you don't even need finiteness for *this* step"). The Lean version confirms that separation cleanly: acyclicity is free, termination-with-a-bound is not. Which means what's *not* yet here is Corollary 2.2's actual numeric bound ($3^{n^2}-1$) — that needs `BoardState n`'s `Fintype` instance plus the observation that a no-repeat history is injective into a finite type, so its length is capped by the type's cardinality. That's a natural next lemma, and it's a short one.

**`lemma_4_2` — fully proved, but it's quietly proving a different, weaker claim than the essay's Lemma 4.2, and the file is honest about this in the comments, which I want to make sure gets carried into any writeup.**

The proof itself is correct: given `h_libs`, it builds the singleton regions `{e1}`/`{e2}` as witnesses, discharges `isEnclosed` for each by unfolding to the two componentwise conditions and using `he1`/`he2` directly, and closes via `OrderHom.le_gfp`. No gaps in the Lean logic.

But look at what `h_libs` actually assumes: *every* liberty of *every* stone in $c$ is either $e_1$ or $e_2$ — i.e., $c$ has **no liberties at all besides the two eyes, right now, in this static position**. The essay's actual Lemma 4.2 argument is dynamic: $c$ may have other outside liberties too, and the content of the theorem is that *as the opponent fills those over time*, the two eyes survive because no single move can fill both, so $c$ never reaches zero liberties. That "opponent fills at most one eye per move, forever, as play continues" reasoning — the actual combinatorial heart of the two-eyes theorem — is exactly what `h_libs` allows the proof to skip. Once you assume the liberty set is *already* just $\{e_1, e_2\}$, the theorem becomes a direct unfolding of `benson_f`'s definition with no game dynamics involved at all, which is why the proof needed no induction over moves, no reference to the `LegalTransition`/`R` machinery from Section 5, nothing.

So: correct proof of a real but narrow special case ("a fully-enclosed two-eyed group with literally only those two liberties is alive"), not yet a proof of the general claim ("a two-eyed group, however many other liberties it currently has, is alive"). The comment in the file (lines 102–105) says this plainly, which is the right way to handle it — I'd just make sure that caveat travels with the theorem name wherever it gets cited, since "Lemma 4.2, formally verified in Lean" and "a strengthened special case of Lemma 4.2, formally verified in Lean" are different claims to make in the essay's front matter.

**What it would take to close that gap, concretely:** you'd need to hook into the Section 5 machinery that's already sitting right below it — state the claim as an invariant preserved by `LegalTransition`: if $c \subseteq$ (some game state's stones) has two disjoint candidate eyes and *at most one* non-eye liberty gets removed per move (which follows from a move being a single stone placement), then after any single legal transition, $c$ still has $\geq 1$ liberty. That's an induction over `ReachablePath`, using `history_length_increases`'s sibling machinery for the move-by-move step, rather than a one-shot unfolding of `benson_f`. It's real work — probably the most substantial single lemma left in this whole project — but it's exactly the piece that would upgrade this from "a verified special case" to "the actual theorem."

**On your broader question — what's realistically verifiable here going forward:**

- **Tractable, similar-sized to what's done:** the $3^{n^2}-1$ bound (Fintype cardinality argument, short); `adj_symmetric`/`adj_irreflexive` (genuinely mechanical `Int.natAbs` case work); $D_4$ acting on the board — Mathlib has dihedral groups and this would compose cleanly with a `SimpleGraph` automorphism argument.
- **Tractable but substantial:** the dynamic strengthening of Lemma 4.2 above; formalizing an actual `isCapture`/move-application function instead of leaving $R$ abstract, so that `LegalTransition` isn't just "some relation with the right shape" but concretely *the* Go move relation.
- **Hard, and I'd be honest that it may not be worth attempting:** Section 5's CGT/surreal endgame material. Mathlib does have `Surreal` numbers (`Mathlib.SetTheory.Surreal`), so the *values* exist as formal objects — but the essay's own Remark on §5 already concedes that the direct-sum decomposition itself fails under ko threats and shared liberties, i.e., the hypothesis under which CGT applies to Go isn't a theorem, it's an assumption you check position-by-position. Formalizing that would mean formalizing "this specific position's regions are independent," which isn't a general theorem to prove once — it's a decidable-but-position-specific fact, closer to a verified computation than a piece of the algebraic framework. I'd treat that section as staying at the essay/prose level rather than chasing a Lean formalization that wouldn't actually generalize.
